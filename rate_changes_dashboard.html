US Electricity Rates: Where the Affordability Issues Are

National and state averages can hide a lot. This dashboard goes from big-picture utility spending down to the utility-by-utility numbers that actually show up on people's bills.
All dollar figures are adjusted for inflation (2024 dollars using CPI-U). Rate data: EIA Form 861. Cost data: FERC Form 1 (investor-owned utilities only).

What are investor-owned utilities spending on?
Investor-owned utilities (IOUs) serve about 70% of US electricity customers. Their spending has two parts: capital investments (building things) and operating costs (running things). Both flow into customer rates.
Capital spending tripled from $40B to $118B/year since 2001. The biggest areas: distribution (poles, wires, transformers — $52B), transmission (long-distance lines — $28B), and other generation (wind, solar, batteries — $13B, up from nearly zero). This capital goes onto the "rate base" — utilities earn a guaranteed return on it, which is the main reason bills are climbing.
Operating costs totaled $282B in 2023. Purchased power (33%) and fuel (13%) are the volatile pieces — they swing with natural gas prices and wholesale markets. Depreciation (17%) nearly doubled since 2001, which is the annual bill for all that capital spending above.

The real picture: how individual utilities compare
State averages can be misleading. Within the same state, one utility might have cut rates 15% while another raised them 40%. Below is every utility selling 500+ GWh/year (that's 364 utilities covering 87% of all US electricity).

The pattern: investor-owned utilities (red) averaged +6.5% real rate increases. Cooperatives (green, −7.1%) and municipal utilities (blue, −5.6%) actually got cheaper on average. The biggest swings come from retail marketers in deregulated states.
